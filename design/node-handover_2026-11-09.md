# Opening another node without pairing again: a design (2026-11-09)

Status: design, decided by Anthony on 2026-11-09 (his answers are in section 13). Revised after an independent review the same day. Nothing here is built yet; the build goes slice by slice, each with an independent review. Decision: D132 (`project-log/decisions/D132-node-handover.md`), decided.
Written for Anthony (owner). Plain words first; the builders' detail follows.

## 0. In plain words

Today, when you tap Open on another node in the Nodes list, that node asks for its own PIN, because every node pairs its own devices. You asked that a device already paired on one node, and allowed to see the others, should just open them. This design does that without ever putting your root key on a node. When you tap Open, the node you are on (call it A) checks that you are still paired and allowed, makes a one-time ticket that lives for a minute, and your browser hands it to the other node (B). B phones A once, over the secure connection, to ask "is this ticket good, and who is it for?", and A answers once. B then signs you in as a device of its own, with the same role or lower, never higher. If anything is off (ticket old, A switched off, a key that does not match, a node you did not approve) you land on the PIN screen as you do today, with one plain sentence saying why. Only Owners and Operators paired over https carry across; guests, presenter codes, controller codes and remote support never do. Two nodes trust each other only when you have said so: either both are on a fleet list that you sign once on your computer with your root, or the Owner of a node accepts the other node once by its key fingerprint, shown on both panels. Having a certificate from your root is not enough, because a stolen, lent or retired node still has one. An Owner who is carried to an approved node is an Owner there and can do everything, which includes making permanent ways in (showing the PIN, making codes, adding or removing devices): that is what you chose, and section 4 says it plainly. Operators and below are never raised. A carried device stays signed in for 24 hours after the node it came from last answered. If a node is stolen or retired, an Owner presses "Remove a node from this cluster" on any node: that node is refused everywhere at once, and every device carried from it is signed out on every node, without re-signing anything on your computer (section 9). Only an Owner can change the "Accept devices from other nodes" switch, and that switch is the lock. Your six answers are in section 13.

## 1. Where things stand (facts read from master 63b16a4, 2026-11-09)

- Pairing is per node (D7, D80). A token is 192 random bits, only its SHA-256 is stored (`pvj/auth.py`). It travels as the cookie `__Host-pvj_token` over https (Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite=Strict, no Domain) or as a Bearer header for scripts (`pvj/server.py`). A cookie belongs to one host name, so a token on node-01 is never sent to node-02: that is why each node asks again.
- Roles are stored as `view` (Guest), `live` (Operator), `full` (Owner). A device record has an optional `via` (`code`, `controller`, `recovery`), and `secure: true` when its token was made over https (D79).
- D79: your root CA is made by `tools/boxcert.py` on your computer, its key never goes on a node. Each node has its own key and a certificate signed by the root, and pins the root (`root.pem`). `HttpsBox._issued_by_stored_root` already checks a certificate against the pinned root with `openssl verify`, dates left out. `httpsbox.name_matches` already says whether a certificate's names cover an address. The node's key is `key.pem` in `<state>/tls`, which only the panel's account reads. D79 also says a stolen card can pretend to be that node until its certificate ends: "the root alone" is therefore not proof that a node is still yours.
- D127 (discovery, step 2a, PR 224): each node announces `id`, `name`, `tier`, `version`, `https` over mDNS, and `pvj/fleet.py` lists the others. The node id is public. It is a hint for where to knock, never a credential, and a cloned card shares its twin's id.
- Step 2b (the node switcher, PR 238): Open is a plain `https://address[:port]/` link in a new tab with `noopener noreferrer`. Its rule "nothing of this node (token, PIN, the page address) goes with it" changes here in one narrow way: a ticket goes, never the token or the PIN.
- Step 2c (signed enrollment through your root) is not built. The fleet proposal of 2026-11-07 (outside the repo; D127 quotes it) says "the owner's own root certificate signs a device's enrollment". This design reads 2c as signing nodes, not devices (section 3.3).
- The page's content security policy has `connect-src 'self'` and `form-action 'self'`, so the panel cannot call another node with `fetch`, nor post a form to one, today. The form post is allowed on purpose in section 5.
- The Pi has no battery clock. `HttpsBox.clock_trusted()` says when a node's clock was set from the network. Nothing here needs two nodes to agree on the time.
- The event log (D125) has fixed kinds (`boot`, `player`, `renderer`, `update`, `projector`, `network`, `signin`, `scene`), 500 lines, Owner-only.
- Step 3 (configurations pushed between nodes, profiles) is not approved and nothing here prepares it beyond the fleet list.

## 2. What is being asked, and what it is not

Asked: "Once one node ... has been paired with a user's device, if they are allowed to see and access other nodes, they should be able to just open the other nodes without having to pair with each one."

In scope: a device paired on A that may see the Nodes list (Owner or Operator, over https, not remote support) opens B signed in.
Not in scope: pairing a new device, copying settings or looks between nodes (step 3), a shared device list, guests, joining by code, controllers, anything over plain http.

The cookie is per host, so every scheme has to put a credential in front of B's origin once. That is why a shared device list does not remove the hand-over step (option 2 below).

## 3. Trust between nodes

### 3.1 Three options

**Option 1: a ticket that A signs with its own key, B checks the signature.**
- Good: B does not need to reach A at the time.
- Against: Python's standard library cannot check a signature, so B would run `openssl` on network data at every sign-in. Freshness needs both clocks, and a Pi with no battery clock is wrong by minutes or years until it learns the time. One-time use lives only in B's memory and is forgotten at a restart. Nothing tells B that A removed the device a minute later. The node's TLS key would also sign a message format, which is key reuse across uses.

**Option 2: a shared device list, synced between enrolled nodes.**
- Good: no calls at sign-in time.
- Against: it does not remove the hand-over step, because the browser still has to give B's origin a credential once, so the long-lived token itself would travel in a form. Every node would hold every device's token hash, so one stolen card exposes the fleet's device list. It needs conflict rules, delete tombstones, an authenticated sync channel and a change to D7. It is step 3's "hub or signed copy" question in disguise.

**Option 3 (recommended): a ticket A keeps, and B calls A back to redeem it.** The ticket is 128 random bits that mean nothing alone. A remembers it (memory only) with the device, the node it is for, and a lifetime counted on A's monotonic clock. B takes the ticket from the browser and asks A over a verified https connection: "is this one good, for me?" A answers once and forgets the ticket.
- Good: no signature code. Freshness and single use are decided in one place, by a clock that only counts seconds. Revocation is live. A can later tell B "this device is gone" (section 8).
- Against: B must reach A while the browser waits (a second or two). The Nodes list already needs the nodes to reach each other, and a Wi-Fi with client isolation stops both. If A is unreachable, the tap falls back to the PIN screen with a sentence.

Recommendation: option 3.

### 3.2 How a node knows it is talking to its peer

Every peer check, in either direction, does all of the following, in order. A failure at any step ends the call and writes an event (section 10).
1. **Find the peer.** The caller has the peer's id, never an address from the browser. It tries every address its own discovery list holds for that id (a node may have an Ethernet and a Wi-Fi address), one after the other, and accepts the first whose certificate passes steps 2 to 6. If the id is not in the list it runs one scan (the cached rate limit of `fleet.py` applies) and tries again. Only private, link-local and loopback addresses are tried, as `fleet.py` already does.
2. **Chain to the root.** The certificate from the handshake chains to the pinned root, by the existing `_issued_by_stored_root` (openssl verify, fixed arguments, `-purpose sslserver`, the root's name constraints, dates left out).
3. **Key possession.** The handshake itself proves the peer holds the certificate's private key.
4. **The address is covered.** The certificate's names cover the address that was connected to (`name_matches`). A certificate for another node's name does not pass for this address.
5. **The id is bound to the key.** The hash of the peer's public key must be the one that the trust record gives for that id: the signed fleet list (3.3) or a node pairing (3.4). A key that changed is refused, not trusted on first sight. This closes the gap that the id is public: an id only counts together with its key.
6. **Not run out.** When `clock_trusted()` is true on the caller, a peer certificate whose end date has passed is refused and logged ("node-02's certificate ended on <date>"). When the clock is not trusted, dates are not judged, as D79 does.
7. **The list is current.** Every peer call carries the caller's fleet-list serial (or "pairing"). A peer with a lower serial is refused. A node that hears a higher serial fetches the list from that peer, verifies it against the pinned root and adopts it (3.3).

There is no lower tier: the shared root alone is never enough (3.3, 3.4).

### 3.3 The fleet list, and what an enrolled node is (step 2c)

An enrolled node is a node named in the **fleet list**: a small file that you sign on your computer with the root. The root key never leaves your computer.

```
fleet.json   {"v":1, "fleet":"<16 hex>", "name":"Anthony's rig", "serial":3, "issued":"2026-11-09",
              "nodes":[{"id":"<node id>", "name":"node-01", "key":"<sha-256 of the node's public key>", "carry":"full"},
                       {"id":"...", "name":"leyline", "key":"...", "carry":"live"}]}
fleet.sig    your root's signature over the bytes  "nxlx-fleet-v1\n" + fleet id + "\n" + serial + "\n" + the bytes of fleet.json
```

- The fleet id and the serial are inside the signed bytes under a domain prefix, so a signature made for something else cannot pass as a list, and a list cannot be moved to another fleet.
- `key` is the hash of the node's public key. A yearly renewal that reuses the key (D79) keeps it; a new key means signing the list again, on purpose.
- `carry` is the highest role this node gives to a device that arrives from another node on the list: `full`, `live` (Operator) or `none` (this node takes nobody in). Your rig says `full`, a client's node says `live` or `none`. **Carry needs both nodes on the same signed list.**
- `serial` only counts up. A node refuses a lower serial than the one it holds. Dates are shown, never judged.
- Making it: `python3 tools/boxcert.py fleet add node-01.local` asks that node for its fleet card (`GET /api/fleet/card`: id, name, key hash; the first two are already announced), checks it against the certificate chain on your computer, and adds a row. `fleet remove`, `fleet sign` (asks the passphrase, raises the serial) and `fleet show` complete it.
- **Where a node keeps it.** In a file `fleet.json` and `fleet.sig` beside `tls/` in the state folder (mode 0640, written by rename), never in `settings.json`. It is left out of settings export and import, of the diagnostics file and of every API answer except the Owner's own status of it (id, name, serial, node names, no keys). A test like D79's walks every GET, the diagnostics file and an export, and finds the file's contents nowhere.
- **Passing a newer list on.** A node that hears a higher serial in a peer call (step 7 of 3.2) fetches the list from that peer over the checked connection, verifies the signature against its own pinned root and that its own id and key are in it, stores it, and logs it. Nothing is trusted from the peer except the signed bytes. Decided (question 5): this is on, so you load a new list once. A list may also carry `"clear": [key hashes]`, a signed lifting of denies (section 9), which only your root can make.
- A node is **enrolled** when it holds a current list that names it. Two nodes are **in the same fleet** when both lists have the same fleet id and name the other.
- This is "signed enrollment through his own root" for nodes. Devices are not enrolled: a node vouches for a device by the hand-over. Signing every device would put your root passphrase on the road for each new phone.

### 3.4 The alternative for one pair: node pairing by fingerprint

For two nodes with no fleet list (a rig of two, or a friend's node), the Owner of B can **accept A once**, by the fingerprint of A's key: B shows "node-01 asks to be trusted: key 3F:9A:... (check that node-01 shows the same)". The same number is shown on node-01's panel. The Owner at B types or confirms it, over https, on a device paired at B. B then keeps a pairing record {id, name, key hash, carry} (carry chosen then, `full`, `live` or `none`), beside `tls/`, with the same exclusions as the list. It is symmetric only if A's Owner accepts B too; a one-sided pairing lets A's devices into B but not B's into A. The record lists on both panels; either Owner removes it with a tap, and removing it signs out every `via: node` device from that peer within the next lease check.

It carries the same roles as the list (section 4) and the same checks (3.2). It is the substitute for a list, not for the root: nothing carries on the root alone. Decided (question 1): the list plus this one-time acceptance, shown on both panels like pairing. **Approved** below means: on the signed list, or accepted by fingerprint, and not on the deny list (section 9).

## 4. Roles

The rule, in one line: **the role on B is the lowest of what the device has on A, what A may vouch for, and B's carry for A. It is never higher than on A, and there is no hand-over unless both nodes are on the same fleet list or each has accepted the other by fingerprint.**

| Case | On A | On B |
|---|---|---|
| Approved peer, B's carry is `full` | Owner | Owner, everything (see "What a carried Owner can do") |
| Same list, B's carry is `live` | Owner | Operator |
| Approved peer, B's carry is `full` or `live` | Operator | Operator |
| Approved peer, B's carry is `none` | any | nothing: the PIN screen |
| A peer on the deny list (section 9) | any | nothing, and its carried devices are signed out |
| Node pairing (3.4) | role as above, capped at the carry chosen | as above |
| Not on the same list, not paired | any | nothing: the PIN screen |
| B's accept switch off, or B has no https | any | nothing |
| Guest, or a device paired by a join code or a link | Guest or presenter | nothing, never |
| A device paired from a controller's code (MIDI) | any | nothing, never |
| A remote support login | Owner by role | nothing, never |

Which devices carry: role Owner or Operator, `secure: true`, and a record with no `via`, or `via` equal to `recovery`. Excluded: Guests, `via` `code`, `via` `controller`, `via` `node` (no chaining: C asks A), and every remote support login, whose routes go in `support.REMOTE_DENY` with the reason "support stays at the node it was given to".

**What a carried Owner can do (decided).** On an approved node, a carried Owner is an Owner and can do everything, with no exceptions. **The consequence, plainly: a carried Owner can create permanent access on B.** He can show or rotate B's PIN, make join codes, controller codes, guest links and invites, add devices, remove or demote B's local Owner, change the root, the certificate, the list and the switch, or reset B. Removing the carried device, or the node it came from (section 9), signs that device out; it does not undo what it made. The protection is the approval, not a restriction: only a node you approved (on the signed list, or accepted by fingerprint by B's own Owner) can carry anybody, an Owner on A is only as trustworthy as A, and a node that stops being trusted is removed with one action (section 9). To see what happened afterwards, the event log and the journal name the carried device ("Anthony iPad via node-01") on every such action, and People lists what exists. The rules that stay: nothing carries from an unapproved node; Operators and below are never raised; guests, presenters, controller codes and remote support never carry; a `via: node` device does not chain to a third node.

B's record is an ordinary device of B (name "device name, via node-01", role as above, `via: node`, `secure: true`, `from: {node, device}`), so People and codes lists it and the Owner at B can remove it. A device that taps Open again on the same B gets the same record (matched by A's node id and device id) with a new token. **At most 8 `via: node` records per source node**: the ninth tap from a different device of the same source replaces nothing and is refused with a sentence, so one node cannot fill B's list. They count in the shared pool beside guests (`_room_for`).

## 5. The flow in the panel

Preconditions for the Open row: B is in A's discovery list with https on; A has https loaded; this device is Owner or Operator, `secure`, not remote; and A has a trust record for B (list or pairing) that passes the check below. If not, Open does what it does today and a line says why in words ("You will be asked for this node's PIN").

1. **Tap.** The panel opens an empty tab at once, named for the node (`window.open('', 'nxlx-' + id)`, inside the tap so no popup blocker objects), sets its `opener` to null, and shows "Signing in to node-02..." in it.
2. **POST to A for the ticket.** `POST /api/handover/ticket` on A: JSON, the request header, same-origin cookie, body `{"to": "<B's id>", "proof": "<hash of a value the page just made>"}`. The panel sends an id, never an address. A checks, in order: the device may carry (section 4); A holds a trust record for B and B's carry is not `none`; **B passes every step of 3.2** (A connects to B, so a ticket is never made toward a host that only announced B's id); and **B's certificate covers the address that A will hand to the browser**, so the browser's own check will pass there too. A throttles tickets (6 a minute per device) and makes a ticket: 128 random bits, kept in memory with the device id, B's id and key hash, the proof hash, and an end time on A's monotonic clock, **60 seconds**. A answers `{"ticket", "action": "https://B:port/handover", "origin": "https://A-origin"}`. The address is the one A just verified.
3. **Form POST to B.** The page builds a form in memory (`method=POST`, `enctype=application/x-www-form-urlencoded`, `action` the answer, `target` the named tab, hidden fields `ticket`, `secret` (the value whose hash was the proof) and `from` (A's node id)) and submits it. The ticket is in a JSON answer over TLS, in the page's memory, and in a POST body over TLS: **never in an address, query string, fragment, history, Referer (`Referrer-Policy: no-referrer`), access log or journal.** The page's content security policy gains `form-action 'self' https://B:port` for each node the Nodes view may open, built on the server from the same list and checked against the shape `CSP_ORIGIN` allows. **A node that appeared after the page loaded** is not in the policy, so the browser would block the form: the panel notices (it asked the node list at the tap, section 6 of D127 says never on a timer) and reloads the page once, which carries the new policy, before it shows Open for that node; until then the row says "Reload the panel to open this node".
4. **B redeems with A.** `POST /handover` exists on the TLS listener only (plain http gets 404 with the https address, no redirect). It takes one form, 2 KB at most. It checks that it accepts hand-overs, and that the **`Origin` header of the form POST equals A's https origin** as A returned it (a request with another Origin, or none from a browser that sends one, is refused: this is what stops another site from posting a ticket to B in your browser). It finds A by id (3.2, every address, first that passes), and calls A's `POST /api/handover/redeem` with `{"ticket", "secret", "to": B's id, "key": B's key hash, "serial": ...}`. A does the lookup and the delete in one step **under A's lock**: the ticket is removed whether or not the rest passes, so two calls can never both win. A then checks that the node and key are those it made it for, that the hash of `secret` is the proof, and that the device is still paired, still Owner or Operator and still `secure`. It answers `{"name", "role", "lease", "origin"}`. **B makes the call once.** If it times out, B does not retry: the ticket is probably already spent; the tab shows the PIN screen with the sentence and the user taps Open again (a new ticket). B lowers the role by its own carry, never raises, and makes or refreshes the record.
5. **B answers a page, not a redirect.** B answers 200 with a tiny page and `Set-Cookie: __Host-pvj_token=...; Secure; HttpOnly; SameSite=Strict; Path=/`. The page's one script (a file of B's own) runs `location.replace('/')`. A redirect would not do: the move after a cross-site POST counts as cross-site, so a SameSite=Strict cookie would not be sent. **B never silently replaces a cookie that is already valid at B with one of a lower or other identity**: if the form arrives with a valid B cookie of a higher role (for example an Owner paired at B, tapping Open from a node where he is only an Operator), B does not set the new cookie; it answers a one-line page, "You are already signed in here as Owner. Switch to the Operator from node-01? [Switch] [Keep]", whose button is a same-origin POST. If the cookie is of the same or a lower role, B replaces it quietly.
6. **B's panel opens signed in.** On any failure in steps 2 to 5 the tab ends at B's PIN screen with the node's sentence ("node-02 did not accept the hand-over: the ticket had run out. Use the PIN.") and the failure is logged (section 10). The back button never re-sends the ticket: the history entry was replaced, and the ticket is gone from A anyway.

**The proof.** A binds a ticket to the browser tab that asked for it: A keeps only the hash, the page keeps the value, and B needs both. A ticket that leaks without the value cannot be redeemed. Single use and the 60 seconds are the first guard; the proof is the second.

**The Fire tab launcher and installed apps.** An installed web app on iOS (standalone) may send `window.open` to Safari, whose cookie jar is not the app's: the hand-over then signs in to B in Safari, not in the app. That is how Open behaves today. The locked launcher tablet (D131, branch firetab-log) runs in its own WebView; whether `window.open` works there is a row to try (HO6), and a launcher-locked device would need the ticket route allowed explicitly. This design does not decide that.

## 6. Where the checks live

- New module `pvj/handover.py` (about 300 lines, standard library): the peer client (3.2), the ticket table (memory, at most 64, monotonic end times), the lease table (section 8), the fleet and pairing readers. A short worker thread does the lease checks and sleeps otherwise.
- Routes on the TLS listener only: `POST /api/handover/ticket` (Operator and up, `secure`, not remote), `POST /handover` (no device; a form), `POST /api/handover/redeem` and `POST /api/handover/lease` (peer calls; no cookie; need a ticket or lease that only A and the right B hold), `POST /api/handover/poke` (no authority), `GET /api/fleet/card` (public, the facts mDNS announces plus the key hash), and the Owner routes for the list, pairings and the accept switch. Each gets a row in `policy`, and in `REMOTE_DENY` where a support session could reach it.
- Peer calls: private, link-local and loopback addresses only, 3 seconds to connect, 6 in all. **One outbound call at a time per peer id**, not one for the node, so a slow or dead peer cannot starve a hand-over with another one. Calls to peers are counted as failures or successes for the throttles of section 7.

## 7. What the switches do

- **Accept devices from other nodes** (Owner, at that node, Setup > Nodes on this network). Off by default, kept in `settings.json` as `fleet.accept` (a plain on or off, in `BOX_OWN` so an import never brings it, gone at a factory reset). Off: `POST /handover` answers the PIN screen, "this node does not take devices from other nodes". Turning it off removes every `via: node` device at once. It does not hold the list or the pairings, so those stay.
- **Owner access only over the secure connection** (D79). A hand-over never needs it and never loosens it. Tickets, redemption, the cookie and the move exist over https only. The new device is marked `secure: true` (its token was made over https), so with the switch on B does not refuse it as "paired over plain http". On A, a device paired over plain http does not carry at all, switch or no switch, because its token may have been listened to. The switch protects the start of the chain: the first node pairs you over https.
- **Locking pairing (decided).** Only an Owner can lock pairing, and the lock is the switch above, "Accept devices from other nodes"; there is no separate button. I found no other switch of that name. The nearest are the PIN guessing lockout (20 wrong PINs in 10 minutes shuts all pairing for a minute, `Auth.pairing_locked`) and the guest-controls lock (D80). Neither affects a hand-over: no PIN is typed, so nothing counts as a guess; the guest lock concerns Guests, who never carry. Only an Owner (a carried Owner too) can change the switch.
- **Throttles.** A bad ticket, bad secret or bad handle has its own throttle at B and at A: 10 **failures** a minute for the node and 5 a minute per source address. Failures count, attempts do not, so a flood of good hand-overs cannot lock the room out and a flood of bad ones cannot hide among them. Good ticket requests are limited per device (6 a minute) as in section 5. A poke with a handle the node does not know costs a table lookup and nothing else: no call, no log line beyond a counter shown on the status.

## 8. Revocation

A device removed on A: A's record goes, and with it every ticket made for it (a ticket is checked against the device list at redemption). The records already made on other nodes go too, in a time you can say out loud.
- **Lease.** At redemption A gives B a lease handle (128 random bits); B stores its hash in the record. While the device is in use, B asks A `POST /api/handover/lease {handle, serial}` at most every 10 minutes (and at once on a poke): "is this device still paired, and with what role?" Gone: B removes its record (the existing `revoke` path: presence, OSC address included). Role lower: B lowers it. Role higher: ignored (a higher role needs a new hand-over).
- **Poke.** When A removes a device it knows it handed over, it sends each such node `POST /api/handover/poke {handle}`. The poke has no authority: B reacts only by asking A, so a forged poke can cause one extra question. A keeps which nodes each device was handed to **in memory** and writes it to the device record **at most once a day** (like `seen`), so a restart can lose a day of that set; the lease check covers the gap within 10 minutes of use.
- **A switched off or unreachable.** B keeps the device until **24 hours** after the last good answer from A, or until B's Owner removes it. The answer is the only thing that renews it. A hand-over refreshes the record. The real limit on a stolen A is not these 24 hours but removing that node: section 9.
- **Everything removed** (sign out every device, factory reset): the next lease check on each node removes the hand-over devices.
- **A node removed from the list or pairing**: its devices fail their next check and go within 10 minutes of use (the peer no longer passes 3.2 step 5).
- **Role change on A**: the lease answer carries it; B follows it downward at the next check.
- **Log out** in B's panel removes B's record only; one more tap on Open signs in again.

## 9. Removing a node from this cluster

A node that is stolen, retired, lent back or re-imaged still has a valid key and a certificate from your root. Dates do not save you (the Pi's clock is not trusted, certificates last a year). Anthony chose the simple cure (question 6): an Owner presses **Remove a node from this cluster** on any node, and re-signing the list is optional tidying afterwards.

**The action.** On any node, an Owner (a carried Owner too) opens Setup > Nodes on this network, picks a node from the approved set (the list and the fingerprint acceptances, with names, whether or not the node is on the network now), reads "node-03 will be refused by every node in this cluster, and every device it carried will be signed out everywhere", and confirms. The node does, at once and locally:
1. Adds the node's key hash to its **deny list** (`deny.json` beside `tls/`, 0640, written by rename, kept out of export, import and diagnostics like the list). An entry holds the key hash, the name, the node that issued it, and a count for ordering; dates are for display only.
2. Signs out every `via: node` device whose source is that node, and removes its fingerprint acceptance, if any.
3. Sends the entry to every approved peer it can reach (checked as in 3.2).

**How the deny spreads.** A peer that receives a deny from any approved peer (one that passes 3.2 and is not itself denied) adds it, does step 2, and passes it on to its own approved peers. Every peer call also carries a short digest of the deny list; a peer that sees a different digest exchanges the missing entries, so a node that was off catches up the next time it talks to anybody. **Only denies are accepted from a peer, never an allow.** A deny can only reduce trust, which is why one approved peer may issue it. Nothing a peer sends can remove a deny, add a node to the approved set, or raise a role.

**What a deny does.** The denied key hash is refused as a peer in both directions: no ticket is made for or from it, its redeem and lease calls are refused, its pokes are ignored, and it is not offered a list. Deny wins over the signed list and over a fingerprint acceptance. A node never denies its own key; one that learns of a deny for its own key says so on its status ("this node has been removed from the cluster") and keeps working alone. Nothing changes for local use: the PIN, local devices, guests and every local feature work as before.

**How long it lasts.** Until (a) a newer signed list that leaves the node out is loaded (the entry is then redundant and is dropped), or (b) an Owner removes the deny on purpose, at one node, with the node's fingerprint typed back ("Allow again"), or (c) a newer signed list lifts it with `clear`. A newer list that still names the node does not lift a deny; only `clear` or an Owner does.

**Re-signing the list** (`fleet remove`, `fleet sign`) is still possible. It makes the removal permanent in the file you keep and drops the deny entries it makes redundant. It is no longer the cure, and nothing waits for it.

**A stolen node, step by step.** (1) On any node, press Remove a node from this cluster for it. Devices it carried are signed out on every node you can reach, and on the others when they next talk to a peer. (2) Follow D79 ("If a box or its SD card is stolen"): a new key on the remaining nodes if the name is still in use, and a different host name for any replacement. (3) When convenient, sign a new list without it. A **re-imaged** node has a new id and key: it is a new node, approved by the list or by fingerprint like any other, and the old entry is removed the same way.

**A node that is off when the deny is pressed** hears it the next time it talks to any approved peer; until then it still trusts the removed node. Press the action on that node too if it matters before then. When `clock_trusted()` is true, a peer certificate that has run out is refused and logged as well (3.2 step 6).

**The abuse case: a malicious or compromised approved node denies everyone.** It can push a deny for every other node. That is a denial of service on hand-overs only, and a bounded one: nobody is locked out of any node, PIN pairing and every local device keep working, and people open the other nodes with the PIN as they do today. An Owner recovers like this:
1. The fact is loud. A node that takes a deny from a peer writes an event and shows Owners a banner: "node-03 removed node-01 and node-02 from this cluster". More than one entry from one peer in a minute makes the banner red.
2. On any good node, press Remove a node from this cluster for the bad one (node-03). The deny spreads, and from then on node-03's pushes are refused.
3. On each node that took node-03's denies, an Owner presses "Allow again" on the wrongly denied nodes, or once on "Allow again everything node-03 denied" (each entry records the issuing node, so this is one tap, with each fingerprint shown). This is the Owner's deliberate action; a peer can never do it.
4. Or, from your computer, sign a list with a higher serial and `clear` for those key hashes (`boxcert.py fleet clear`; this field is a small addition of mine, the one cluster-wide allow, and it needs your root). Load it on one node and it spreads (question 5, yes).
5. Then handle node-03 as a stolen node above.
This does not undo permanent access a compromised Owner already created on other nodes (section 4); that is removed by hand in People.

## 10. The event log and the journal

A new kind `handover` (the `KINDS` tuple is the only change to the events module), plain sentences, `who` the device's name:
- On A, when a ticket is redeemed: "Anthony iPad (Owner) was signed in to node-02." On a refusal at A: "A ticket for node-02 was not made: node-02's key is not the one on the fleet list."
- On B, on success: "Anthony iPad (Owner) signed in from node-01." On refusal: "A hand-over from node-01 was refused: the ticket had ended. / ... node-01's key is not on the fleet list. / ... this node does not take devices from other nodes. / ... node-01 could not be reached. / ... node-01's certificate ended on <date>. / ... node-01 has an older fleet list."
- On removal at B by lease: "Anthony iPad was signed out: node-01 no longer has it paired." On the 24 hours: "... node-01 has not answered for 24 hours."
- A node removed from the cluster, or a removal learned from a peer: "node-03 was removed from this cluster by Anthony iPad (on node-01). 3 devices it carried were signed out." / "node-03 removed node-01 and node-02 from this cluster" (the banner line). An "Allow again": "node-02 was allowed again by Anthony iPad."
- A carried device that shows or rotates the PIN, makes a code or link, or removes a device: the usual line with "via node-01" added.
- A fleet list or pairing loaded, adopted from a peer, or removed: "The fleet list (serial 4, 3 nodes) was loaded / taken from node-01."

The journal gets the same sentences with the device id, never a ticket, handle, secret, token, key or whole certificate. Repeats within 5 minutes collapse as the log does. The two ends write separately; the pair of lines, one on each node, is the trail. It is not a security audit trail (D125).

| Threat | What stops it |
|---|---|
| A stolen ticket (proxy, history, screenshot) | 60 seconds, used once, useless without the `secret` only the page holds, never in an address. Dead at A the moment it is used. |

| A stolen, retired or lent node (valid key and certificate) | There is no lower tier: the root alone carries nothing. It is out as soon as an Owner presses Remove a node from this cluster on any node (section 9), and, when the clock is trusted, when its certificate ends. Until a node has heard the deny, it can pose as one of your nodes to that node. |
| A node posing as B to teach users to type B's PIN into it | A hand-over to it needs a key hash on the list or pairing; and the PIN screen is B's own page: the user reaches it by the address the browser verified. A rogue host with no certificate of yours shows a full-page browser warning, as today. |
| A node whose certificate changed | Same key under a renewed certificate: nothing changes. A new key: the list does not match; hand-overs to and from it are refused with a sentence until you sign again. No trust on first use, no "accept this new key" prompt. |

A compromised B learns the name and role of a device that taps Open on it, and gets no token for any other node. |
| A carried Owner creating permanent access on B | Allowed on purpose (decided): a carried Owner is an Owner, so he can show the PIN, make codes and links, and add or remove devices, and what he makes outlives the lease. The limit is the approval: only nodes you approved can carry anybody, and a bad node is removed in one action (section 9). The event log names the carried device on each such action. A removal signs the carried device out; it does not delete what it made. |
| A removed node still trying | It is on the deny list at every node that has heard (section 9), so it fails 3.2 in both directions; its carried devices are gone. A node that has not heard yet is the gap, closed the next time it talks to any peer. |
| A compromised approved node denying everyone | Bounded to hand-overs; the banner, the removal of the bad node, "Allow again" per node, or a signed `clear` (section 9). Peers never accept an allow, so the bad node cannot undo its own removal. |
| Downgrade to http | The routes exist on the TLS listener only. The cookie is `__Host-` and Secure. A device paired over http does not carry. A node without https offers no hand-over. No redirect from http to https. |
| CSRF on the ticket request | JSON only, the `X-PVJ-Request` header, an `Origin` equal to `Host`, SameSite=Strict cookie. |
| Login CSRF on the form (a site makes your browser post an attacker's ticket and secret) | The form's `Origin` must equal A's https origin as A returned it in the redeem answer. The attacker also needs your browser to hold his own valid ticket and secret. B never overwrites an existing valid cookie of a higher role without a confirm page (section 5, step 5). B's People list names the device. |
| Clock skew (no battery clock) | No decision uses a wall clock. Ticket life and lease checks use each node's monotonic clock; the list uses a serial; dates are judged only when `clock_trusted()` and only to refuse a run-out certificate. |
| Guessing a ticket or a handle | 128 bits each; failures throttled (section 7). |
| A flood of redeem calls used against A | Peer calls need a ticket first, run one at a time per peer id, 6 in all a minute, and go only to addresses in B's own discovery list. |
| OSC and MIDI | A carried Owner device at B has an address, so it passes OSC's "sender must be a paired device" rule (D78) while it is signed in, like any Owner device, and `forget_address` ends it with the lease. MIDI controllers and their codes are not devices and never carry. OSC feedback targets (D82) are per node and not carried. |
| A support helper | Tickets, redemption, the list and the pairings are denied to remote support; a support login does not carry; B refuses `/handover` through the tunnel. |
| What "Owner only over https" does | Section 7: not loosened, not needed, and it makes the chain start on https. |
| Key reuse | The node's TLS key is used for TLS only. Nothing is signed with it. |
| Hostile Wi-Fi | The bytes are TLS. Client isolation stops the call between nodes and the tap falls back to the PIN. |

## 16. Settled in slice 1 (PR 265)

What the fleet list's builder had to choose where section 3.3 was silent. These now stand for the later slices.

1. **The key hash** is the SHA-256 of the DER SubjectPublicKeyInfo of the node's certificate (the value `openssl x509 -pubkey | openssl pkey -pubin -outform DER | openssl dgst -sha256` gives). A renewal that keeps the key keeps the hash. `pvj/fleetlist.key_hash` computes it without openssl.
2. **The signature** is the root's SHA-256 signature in OpenSSL's own DER form, written as base64 text in `fleet.sig`. A node checks it with `openssl x509 -pubkey` and `openssl dgst -sha256 -verify`, over the signed bytes of section 3.3. The checker reads the whole list as strict JSON: unknown or repeated fields, a repeated id, key or name, a carry that is not `full`, `live` or `none`, a version that is not the integer 1, and a file over 64 KiB (64 nodes, 256 clears at most) are refused even when signed.
3. **Equal serials.** A lower serial is refused. The same serial with the same bytes is accepted (a node can be told twice). The same serial with other contents is refused.
4. **Id lengths.** The fleet id is 16 hex digits. Node ids are 32 hex digits, as `pvj/fleet.py` makes them. Key hashes are 64.
5. **The port.** `fleet add HOST` asks `https://HOST:443`; `--port` sets the node's TLS port.
6. **`clear`** exists in the file format and in `boxcert.py fleet clear` from slice 1. The verifier accepts it and keeps it sorted. It has no effect until slice 4d.
7. **Copying is binary.** The signature covers the file's exact bytes. `fleet.json` must travel as a binary file (no line-ending conversion, no editor, no mail program that rewraps). Slices that store the file, or send it between nodes, must keep the bytes.
8. **Extra rules the tool and checker apply.** Two nodes on one list cannot share a key or a name (names compare without regard to case). `--carry` has no default. A node already on the list with another key needs `--replace-key`, and the new key may not be one another row has. `fleet add --cert` takes the key only from a certificate your root signed for a server. The serial restarts never: `sign` takes the higher of the signed list and the working copy, and refuses to sign when the signed pair is gone but the working copy says it was signed, unless `--serial` is given above it. `--serial` stays at least 100000 below the top of the range. The signature file is written before the list file.
9. **Left for slice 2** (done there, see section 17): `GET /api/fleet/card` on the node, with the `policy.NO_GATE` test updates. `fleetlist.make_card` and `parse_card` were ready for it.

## 17. Settled in slice 2 (storage, the card, the approved question)

1. **Where it lives.** `pvj/handover.py` (`Handover`) holds the files beside `tls/` in the state folder: `fleet.json` and `fleet.sig` (the old pair kept as `.prev` until the new one is in place, so a crash between the two renames falls back to the pair that matches), `pairings.json` (the acceptances) and `deny.json`. All mode 0640, written by rename, none in `settings.json`. The switch is `fleet.accept` in the existing `fleet` section of the settings, which was already a box-own section (`boxcare.BOX_OWN`): shown in the diagnostics file as an on or off, never exported, and a file cannot set it. The discovery switch used to overwrite that whole section; it now keeps the other keys.
2. **Routes.** All under `/api/fleet`, the Owner's, over the secure connection only (the server tells the API which listener a request came in on), and refused to a support session (`support.REMOTE_DENY_PREFIX`). `GET /api/fleet/card` is the second route in `policy.NO_GATE`: it asks nobody who they are, answers over TLS only, and says the node's id and name (already announced by mDNS) and the key hash. The request body limit is 64 KiB, so a list near the 64 KiB file limit cannot be posted; real lists are a few KiB.
3. **Taking a list.** The check is `fleetlist.verify` against the pinned root, then two more: the list names this node's id, and with the key of the certificate in use now. A node with no root or no certificate cannot take a list and says why. A refused list changes nothing on disk.
4. **Acceptance by fingerprint.** The node stores {id, name, key hash, carry} only when the typed fingerprint equals the key (spaces, colons, dashes and case do not matter). The candidate's key comes from the caller; in slice 3 the panel gets it from the peer's card over the checked connection, and the fingerprint typed back is what the Owner reads on the other node's panel. The same id with another key is refused until the old acceptance is removed. A denied key cannot be accepted.
5. **`approved(id, key)`.** On the held list: both this node and the peer are on it, the peer with that id and that key, and the carry is THIS node's own row (carry is what a node gives to others). Accepted: the carry chosen at the acceptance. The list wins when both apply. A denied key is never approved. A node is never its own peer. It returns {via, name, carry} or None; a carry of `none` is approved and gives nothing. `carried_role(role, carry)` is the lowest of the device's role and the carry; Guests never carry.
6. **Not here.** The panel screens (the panel session owns app.js; the event kind `handover` needs a label in its `EVENT_KINDS`), tickets, the peer channel, the lease, and the action "Remove a node from this cluster" (the deny file is storage and reading only). Turning the switch off will sign out carried devices when slice 4a makes them.
7. **The floor.** A node that cannot check the files on disk at start (the root briefly missing, the key renewed) still parses them and remembers the fleet and the highest serial, and `take_list` enforces both, including the same-serial-other-bytes rule, until a list checks out. A newer list on disk that does not check out is not replaced by the `.prev` pair; the older pair is used only when no list file is left, or the one left is no newer. Slice 3's adoption of a list from a peer goes through the same `take_list`, so it cannot roll a node back or move it to another fleet.
8. **The card and discovery.** The card is given only while discovery is on. With it off, `boxcert.py fleet add` says so and the owner uses `--cert` and `--id`.
9. **Loading a list.** `boxcert.py fleet push HOST` posts the signed pair over https, with only the owner's root trusted for the connection, using the node's PIN (prompted, never in argv; the device it pairs is logged out again) or an Owner token from `--token-file`.
10. **The slice 3 trap.** When the panel (or anything else) offers an acceptance, `typed` must be what the Owner typed from the OTHER node's own panel, never filled from the same card the `key` came from: a card that lies about the key would then confirm itself. The API takes both as separate fields for that reason.

## 18. Settled in slice 3 (the peer channel)

1. **Where.** `pvj/peer.py` (`Peers`) is the client and the answer to a hello; `POST /api/fleet/peer` is the third route in `policy.NO_GATE` (TLS only, no token). `HttpsBox.chains_to_root(der)` is the check of 3.2 step 2 on a certificate a peer showed (the same `openssl verify` as for this node's own certificate, dates left out).
2. **The checks in order, per address.** Private, link-local or loopback address only; chain to the pinned root; the certificate names the address OR the `.local` name the node announced for that row (an address changes with DHCP and a certificate may name the host only; this step is belt and braces, the key hash below is the real binding); the key hash is one the list or an acceptance gives for that id; a run-out certificate refused only while `clock_trusted()`. A failed check writes an event ("node-02 was not trusted: ..."). Every address held for the id is tried in turn; the first that passes carries the call. The handshake proves key possession.
3. **The serial.** Every call carries the caller's serial or "pairing", its deny digest and the signed list it holds. The answerer takes a newer carried list through `take_list` (so the root's signature, the floor and the fleet pin decide, and who the caller really is does not matter; two tries a minute per sender, a failing list is charged to the sender) and sends its own list when the caller is behind. A caller takes a higher serial's list from the answer the same way, and the peer must still be approved under the new list. A peer that is behind and could not take the list is refused for that call (409); being ahead is never counted as a failure. A list longer than 30000 characters is not carried in a hello (with its quotes and line breaks escaped it would not fit the server's 65536 byte body limit, which is also why a list that large cannot be pushed with `fleet push`); the answer path still brings it to a node that is behind. Until 4a authenticates callers, the event for a list taken from a carried hello says "taken from a hello claiming node-01", not "from node-01".
4. **Two budgets, never mixed.** One outbound call per peer id (a second is refused at once, 429, not queued). Outbound: this node's own failed calls count, 10 a minute for the node and 5 for each target (address and port). Inbound: a bad or unapproved call is charged only to its sender's address (5 a minute); it never touches the node-wide count, so nobody on the network can switch this node's own calls, the Owner's look and accept, or other senders' hellos off. The 6 second limit is one budget across all addresses of a call. Both tables are bounded and drop the least recently hit.
5. **Unknown callers.** An unapproved caller costs a lookup and a count (`unknown_calls` on the Owner's status): no outbound call, no log line, no event.
6. **Denies.** Pull only, and bounded. A hello whose digest differs gets the answerer's entries; the caller takes the ones it lacks and never removes any; the answerer, seeing a different digest, asks the caller back (at most every 10 seconds per node, and twice a minute per sender) so both end with the union. A deny for this node's own key is not stored; the status says "removed from the cluster". An event names who removed whom. Peers may put at most 192 rows in the deny file of 256 (the rest is for the Owner's own removals) and give at most 64 an hour each; names from a peer are cleaned to host names; more than 20 new removals at once, or any that could not be taken, is an event ("node-03 sent 200 removals; this node took 64") and a line on the status. Answers that carry the list or denies are limited to two a minute per sender. The 192 row cap is in the deny file and survives a restart; the hourly window per peer is in memory only and starts again at a restart (the cap still holds).
7. **Accepting by fingerprint, the trap closed in the API.** `POST /api/fleet/accepted` no longer takes a key or a name. The key and name come from the peer's card over a connection that passed the checks above except the key (not known yet), and the card's key must equal the key of the certificate that connection showed. `typed` is compared with that key. The panel must still make the Owner read the fingerprint on the other node, never fill `typed` from `look`.
8. **Retry.** A list on disk that did not check out at start is read again when the root or the certificate has changed (checked on the next ask; nothing is read otherwise). R1: while the older `.prev` pair is in use, or a signature is there without its list, the status keeps a plain line.
9. **Found, for review and slice 4d.** (a) Nothing authenticates the CALLER of a hello (a claim of an approved id and key hash, which are public). The answer gives only what an approved node may hold: the list (names, ids, key hashes, carry levels) when the caller is behind, and the denies. If that is too much, the fix is client certificates in the TLS handshake for this route. (b) A deny that one node lifted comes back from a peer that still holds it, so "Allow again" at one node is undone by the next hello. Slice 4d needs a memory of the lifting (a local list of keys not to take again from peers, cleared by a signed `clear`) or the Owner must lift it on every node.
10. **Allow again that sticks (for slice 4d; not built).** Every deny row gets an integer epoch. "Allow again" writes a LOCAL tombstone for the key with epoch + 1, which is never sent to a peer. A peer's deny for a key is taken only when its epoch is higher than this node's tombstone or row; the digest covers (key, epoch) pairs; equal epochs: the deny wins. A signed `clear` removes rows and tombstones. So a deny lifted on purpose is not brought back by the next hello, a new deny after it (epoch + 2) is, and nothing a peer sends can lift one.
11. **Authenticating the caller (for slice 4a; not built).** Slice 4a moves the peer route to a dedicated TLS listener with `CERT_REQUIRED`, the pinned root as the only CA and the client certificate's key hash checked against the list or acceptance after the handshake, so the caller is proved by the handshake and not claimed. For that, node certificates signed from now on carry `clientAuth` as well as `serverAuth` (`boxcert.py sign`). **A node certificate signed before this change has `serverAuth` only and must be signed again before 4a** (the same names, `boxcert.py renew` with the node's own request; the key may stay, so the key hash on the list does not change).
12. **Not done, on purpose.** (a) A node that is not in the Nodes list yet is not scanned for by this module: the lookup is the Nodes list, which starts its own rate-limited scan when its last one is old, so the next call finds it. (b) The 403 for a caller that is not approved against the 200 for one that is tells a LAN host whether an id and key hash pair is approved; both are public values, so it learns what it already knows, and the answer to an approved claim is the same as it would get from any approved node. 4a's handshake removes it.

## 19. Settled in slice 4a (the ticket and the peer listener)

What the builder of slice 4a had to choose where the sections above were silent, and what changed from slice 3. These now stand for 4b to 4d.

1. **The peer listener.** `PeerServer` (`pvj/server.py`) is a TLS listener on `PVJ_PEER_PORT` (8443 in the unit; none when unset). `HttpsBox.peer_context()` makes its context: the node's own certificate and key, `CERT_REQUIRED`, and the pinned root as the only CA (nothing from the system's store). It answers two POSTs and nothing else: `/api/fleet/peer` (the hello of slice 3, moved here) and `/api/handover/redeem`. The panel's own TLS port no longer answers a peer call at all (the route is gone from `policy.NO_GATE`); it still gives the card, which an Owner reads before a node is accepted.
2. **Who is calling.** The handshake proves the chain to the root and that the caller holds the key. `Peers.authenticate(der, source)` then reads the certificate: it must have the `clientAuth` key usage, must not have run out while this node's clock is trusted, and its public key hash is the caller's key. `handle_peer` and `redeem` look the caller up with that key. The key in the body is only compared with it: a valid node of the root that claims to be another node is refused (a 403 and a count). This closes the gap of section 18, items 9(a) and 12(b). Failures are charged to the sender's address only.
3. **Dates in the handshake.** The context sets OpenSSL's `X509_V_FLAG_NO_CHECK_TIME` (0x200000, bit 21; a Pi has no battery clock; HO9), and the dates, both `not_before` and `not_after`, are judged afterwards by `authenticate`, only while the clock is trusted. A test makes a peer handshake with an expired and a not-yet-valid certificate: it passes while the clock is not trusted and is refused when it is. (The first version of 4a had the flag at bit 33, which OpenSSL ignores, so the handshake did judge dates; found in review.)
4. **The port of the others.** A node calls another on the peer port named in its discovery row, or on `DEFAULT_PEER_PORT` (8443), the same number on every node. It is not announced by mDNS in 4a (that would change the avahi file and the scan); a row of the Nodes list can carry a fourth field when a later slice announces it.
5. **Old certificates.** A node certificate signed before slice 3 has `serverAuth` only. The listener refuses it with a sentence ("signed before nodes called each other and must be signed again"); the same names and the same key, `boxcert.py renew`, so the key hash on the list does not change. The tool has signed `serverAuth, clientAuth` since slice 3.
6. **The ticket.** 32 hex digits (128 bits), kept as a SHA-256 in a table in memory (64 at most), with the device id, the node it is for, the key it was checked against, the hash of the page's secret, A's own origin, and an end time 60 seconds ahead on the monotonic clock. Six tickets a minute per device. A makes it only for a node that passes the whole of 3.2 at the address it hands back (`Peers.panel_address`: the certificate covers that address, or the `.local` name the node announced, which is then what the form is sent to). If B's row on the held list gives `none`, A says no at once; for a node accepted by fingerprint A cannot know and B decides.
7. **Redeem.** `POST /api/handover/redeem` {ticket, secret, to, key, serial}. The look-up and the delete are one step under the lock; the ticket is gone whether or not the rest passes. Then, in order: the ticket had not ended; it was made for this node and this key and the handshake showed that key; the node is still approved (a deny made after the ticket wins); the list serial is not older; the hash of the secret is the proof; the device is still paired, still an Owner or Operator, still `secure`, still no `via` (or `recovery`). The role it answers is the one the device has NOW. A bad ticket, node or secret counts as a failure. There is no count for the whole node, because whoever is on the network can post junk: a refusal to an approved peer counts only against that peer's own counter (20 a minute), and a call from anything else only against its own address (5 a minute). An ended ticket, a gone device or an older list does not count.
8. **The form.** `POST /handover` and `POST /handover/switch` are answered by the panel's TLS handler (not plain http, which gets a 404 that names the https address and does not redirect, and not a support tunnel). One urlencoded form of 2 KB at most. B checks the switch first (nothing is called when it is off), then that the `Origin` is an https origin, that A is approved and gives a carry, and only then calls A once (`Peers.call_once`: no second address after the request was sent, no retry; a refusal from A counts as a failure at B). The `Origin` is compared with the origin A returned; a mismatch spends the ticket and ends at the PIN screen. A page answers, not a redirect: the cookie `__Host-pvj_token` is set (Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite=Strict, Path=/), and `/handover.js` (a file of the panel's own, because the policy forbids inline script) does `location.replace('/')`.
9. **The higher cookie.** When the form arrives with a valid cookie of this node whose role is higher than the one the device would get, nothing is replaced: B keeps the pending hand-over in memory (8 at most, 120 seconds, no record and no token yet) and answers a page with a Switch button, a same-origin POST that needs the `Origin` of this node and the cookie that proves it is that browser. Keep is a link to the panel; the pending one runs out. Equal or lower: replaced quietly.
10. **The record.** `Auth.add_node_device`: name "device, via node-01" (cut to 40), role = the lowest of A's answer and B's carry (`carried_role`), `via: node`, `secure: true`, `from: {node, device}`. The same device of the same node gets the same record with a new token; at most 8 records per source node (`NODE_DEVICES_PER_SOURCE`); they count in the shared pool beside guests (`SHARED_VIA`).
11. **The switch.** Turning "Accept devices from other nodes" off now signs out every `via: node` device at once (it was written down for 4a in section 17, item 6); the list and the acceptances stay. Turning it on does not bring them back. Only an Owner changes it, a carried Owner included (the route is `full`).
12. **Left for 4b to 4d.** The lease (the record keeps A's node id and device id; the redeem answer is an object that can carry more), pokes, the 24 hour lapse, the walk of the route table with a carried Owner, "Remove a node from this cluster". The `handover` event lines of section 10 are written for the ticket and the sign-in; the banner and the removal lines are 4d.
